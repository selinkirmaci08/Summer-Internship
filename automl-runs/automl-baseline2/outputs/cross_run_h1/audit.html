Nineteen runs, one baseline

Six of the nineteen agentic runs produced a model. One of those beats an off-the-shelf AutoML on honest, held-out rows. None of them beats the seasonal naive at one day out. Here is every run, what it produced, and what went wrong where something did.

The feature engineering is carrying the pipeline, and carrying it alone. The training node adds a fifth of an MAE point in one run and loses a tenth in the other — nothing a generic 900-second search would not have found on its own. And the preprocessing node is negative in both runs: handing AutoML the agent's cleaned frame instead of the raw one made things 1.3 to 1.9 points worse. On a single cutoff that is too small to call a real regression, but "not distinguishable from zero" is the most that can be claimed for it. Note that the cleaning also widens the frame—114 columns to 156 in the first run—so it may simply be adding columns the generic recipe then dilutes itself across.

Comparison three · horizon by horizon

Where the long horizons change the verdict

The h=1 table judges both horizon-conditioned runs at the one horizon they were least built for. Each predicts t+h in a single jump from data at the origin, so every horizon is a separate, independent question—and the answer changes completely as h grows. This is the comparison that decides whether either run is worth running at all.

run_20260827T154407Z h = 10 where it overtakes the seasonal naive, and never gives the lead back
run_20260827T154407Z +15.4% skill over the naive at h=364, up from −4.7% at h=1
run_20260901T095109Z h = 240 the first horizon anything beats the naive — and it is AutoML, not the agent
run_20260901T095109Z 20 of 23 horizons won outright by the seasonal naive

run_20260827T154407Z — the value is entirely at the long end

The naive takes the first seven horizons and then falls apart: its MAE climbs from 39.80 at h=1 to 55.96 at h=364, because "the same weekday a year ago" stops carrying any information. The agentic model barely moves—41.67 to 47.35 across the same span—so the gap opens steadily. That flatness is the result: it is not that the model gets good at long horizons, it is that it does not get worse, and the naive does.

The B1 line sits on top of the agentic line at every horizon, within 0.35 MAE from h=1 to h=90, and B1 actually wins six horizons in the 21–90 band. Only past h=270 does the agentic model pull clear, by 0.93 and then 1.27. If the tuning node earns anything anywhere in this project, it is those last two points.

run_20260901T095109Z — the naive holds to half a year

A different story and a worse one. The naive leads at every horizon through h=182, by 39% at h=1 and still by 2% at h=182. The crossover only arrives at h=240, and when it does the winner is B2 preprocessed—generic AutoML on the agent's cleaned frame—not the agent's own model, which takes only h=364.

Note the shape of the naive's line here: it is flat-to-rising from 28.18 up to 60.10, while this run's own model rises from 39.22 to 53.75. Both degrade; the model simply starts so far behind that it needs 240 days of horizon to catch up. Its test window is also shorter and its per-horizon sample shrinks with h (286 dates at h=364, 219 at h=42), so the long-horizon end of this panel rests on fewer days than the equivalent point in the other.

What this changes. run_20260827T154407Z is not a marginal result that happens to clear the naive by 3.7% overall—it loses the short horizons and wins the long ones decisively, and the overall average hides both facts. Its 3.7% is the average of −4.7% and +15.4%. Whether the run is useful depends entirely on which horizon the bakery actually needs.

And the two runs disagree about the whole enterprise. run_20260901T095109Z is the newer, cleaner, better-documented run; it is also the one that cannot beat "look at the same weekday last week" until it is forecasting eight months out. Newer did not mean better here.

All nineteen runs

The stages are the pipeline's own nodes, so the grouping is a real sequence: a run appears under the last node it completed.

01

Stopped at EDA

6 runs
run_20260811T141909Z different problem

Ran on adult.csv—32,561 rows, income classification. Not the bakery task at all; a smoke test of the EDA node on a second dataset. Feasibility passed, F1 recommended for the 24% minority class, run ended there. Nothing to compare.

run_20260811T144500Z correct stop

34 daily rows, 16 November to 24 December 2010— 4.9 weeks. EDA failed feasibility on sample size and halted: too few points to learn a weekly cycle, let alone hold out a split, and one 618-unit day against a median of 394 would dominate any fit. The right call, made for the right reason.

run_20260811T151500Z correct stop

A 149-row 2022 slice. EDA found 56 of 149 rows (37.6%) with fewer than 10 orders, 98.2% of them at zero bread, alongside days like 17 October with 3 orders but 1,001 items and €2,000 revenue. It called the population bimodal rather than noisy and stopped. This is the finding every later run inherits.

run_20260811T160000Z no model

3,809 rows across 13 years—the first slice EDA judged feasible, with enough repeated annual cycles to both learn and validate. It localised the anomaly to 350 rows (9.2%), present every year from 2015 and absent 2010–2014, tying it to the schema switch. Then the run ended without preprocessing anything.

run_20260818T000000Z no model

EDA on the enriched 3,809×28 frame; flagged bread_revenue as collinear with the target and probable leakage, and noted weather missing for ~27% of rows. data/ and models/ are empty—nothing was persisted.

run_20260818T082437Z no model

EDA only, and the sharpest of the six on leakage: it called bread_revenue's near-perfect correlation "a red flag, not a genuine driver—quantity × price", and dismissed num_orders as a same-day outcome rather than a leading indicator. A later run ignored exactly that.

05

Trained, saved, and scoreable

6 runs
run_20260901T095109Z best verified h=1 MAE 36.69 · +9.8% vs AutoML

Why it is good: the only model to beat the AutoML baseline on honest rows, and the only one with a positive R². It is horizon-conditioned, it names 22 columns in must_drop_columns with reasons, and it declares which features are known at forecast time—so same-day revenue and transaction counts never reach it. It completed to an end-model node.
Why it is not good enough: the seasonal naive beats it by 11% here and wins 20 of 21 horizons on its own protocol. Its B1 gap of −0.10 says AutoML matches it on its own features, so the tuning bought nothing. Bias +31.6: it over-forecasts a falling market.

run_20260827T154407Z beats its own baseline own protocol MAE 43.51 · +3.7% vs naive

Why it is good: the only run in the project that beats every AutoML tier and the naive on its own protocol—the sole demonstration that the pipeline can add value a generic search does not find. 56 features, 20 dropped columns, 21 horizons out to 364 days, completed end to end.
Why it looks bad above: two reasons, and the second matters more. h=1 is the horizon it cares least about, and its win lives at the long end the cross-run table cannot reach. But the shared window also sits entirely on the far side of a level break this model never absorbed. Split its own test span at the point the shared window opens: before 2021-07-02 it scores MAE 29.00 with a bias of +7.9, beating the naive by 24%; from 2021-07-02 on it scores 49.73 with a bias of +47.9 and loses. Demand steps down about 35 units in 2021Q3 — quarterly bias runs +7.6, +9.3, then +45.1 — and the model's level never follows. It was a good model measured on the wrong half of its own window.

run_20260820T110000Z duplicate rows h=1 MAE 45.78 · val MAE 38.09

What is wrong: 40 dates appear twice in its feature table with different bread quantities, and the lag chain was computed over row order rather than the calendar—so on those days lag_1 is the same day's other row, not yesterday. Two of the duplicated dates fall in the scoring window and were dropped for every system. It also feeds the model same-day distinct_waitresses. 21 features, no holiday or school-calendar signal at all, and it never evaluated its own test split.

run_20260821T140000Z clean but thin h=1 MAE 52.26 · val MAE 30.89

Why it is honest: the best-designed of the four older runs. A genuinely chronological split, no same-day activity columns, and weather used only as a forecastable proxy.
Why it still loses: 22 features and no calendar knowledge beyond sine/cosine—no holidays, no school terms. Its own inputs are sparse: 1,025 of 2,326 training rows have no weather at all and 413 have no lag_1. Validation 30.9 to held-out 52.3 is the gap that closed-loop tuning on one window cannot see. It never evaluated its own test split either.

run_20260819T090000Z target leakage h=1 MAE 77.24 · val MAE 28.35

What is wrong: its top feature is same-day num_orders at 9.7% importance, followed by pct_ticket_printed, top_payment_mode_count, avg_order_value and total_revenue—all measurements of the day you are trying to forecast, none of them available when you would forecast it. These are precisely the columns the two newest runs list in must_drop_columns, and an earlier EDA had already called num_orders a same-day outcome rather than a leading indicator.
What it cost: validation MAE 28.35 became 77.24 on held-out days—a 2.7× collapse, the worst result in the audit. The leaked columns did not even help: pct_ticket_printed runs at 5.4 in training and 98.5 in test, so the model leaned hardest on the feature that changed most.

run_20260821T130000Z split contaminated · excluded val MAE 21.09 — best-looking, least trustworthy

What is wrong: the split is not chronological. Train spans 21 June 2014 to 8 February 2023 against a test window of 29 May 2015 to 1 March 2022—training rows sit inside and after the test period. It was excluded from the ranking and scored separately to measure the damage: 221 of the 270 scored dates were in its own training set. It scores MAE 20.53 on the days it memorised and 25.82 on the 49 it did not.
Why it matters: its features are clean, its 14 iterations are the second most of any run, and it posts the best validation MAE in the project. Every visible signal says it is the best model here. The split says none of those numbers mean anything.

Method

What was asserted, not assumed

A comparison whose two sides sit on slightly different rows is worse than no comparison, because it still looks like a result. Each of these is checked in code before any prediction is made, and raises rather than scoring a near-match.

✓

No model sees a scored day. Every scored date postdates each model's training and validation windows. The tightest margin is one day, for run_20260901T095109Z; the widest is 230.

✓

The index is a subset of every run's own test partition. Checked run by run, not inferred from the date range.

✓

The baseline's training rows stop before the window opens. The AutoML reference is trained on 3,109 rows through 1 July 2021 and asserted to reach no further.

✓

One truth, not six. The runs disagree about what actually happened on 15 to 40 dates across full history, because they clean differently. One such date—17 January 2023—fell in the window and was dropped. The naive and AutoML references use a median-across-runs series, so neither inherits any single run's cleaning.

✓

Missing lags are scored, not dropped. Every model saw NaNs in its own training rows, so a missing lag is in-distribution. Requiring fully populated rows would have cut the window at 27 December 2021 and discarded the whole second year.

✓

A contaminated run is shown, not hidden. Excluding it silently invites someone to recompute the number later without the caveat.

Limits

What this cannot settle

One cutoff, not nested CV. Both comparisons inherit a single split. Runs landing within a point or two of each other are not separated here.

The cross-run ranking measures window placement as much as modelling. Every system faced the same 2021Q3 level break, so the table is a fair answer to "which of these saved models would have served best over these 270 days". It is not a fair answer to "which run modelled better", because a run's score depends heavily on how far its training window sits from that break, and no run chose its window with this comparison in mind. Removing a single constant offset from each system makes the point: run_20260901T095109Z beats run_20260827T154407Z by 12.0 MAE raw and by 0.66 de-biased — the whole gap is level anchoring, and 0901 is better anchored only because its training window starts in 2016 rather than 2010. On shape the two are indistinguishable. Note that de-biasing with the test mean is an oracle diagnostic, not a deployable correction. Separating the two runs properly would need both pipelines retrained on one cutoff placed clear of mid-2021.

h=1 flatters short memory. The naive winning at one day out is consistent with run_20260827T154407Z's own per-horizon result, where the naive took 7 of 21 horizons—all at the short end. Nothing in the cross-run table speaks to the long horizons, and the four older runs cannot be asked: they never modelled them.

Thirteen runs are absent by construction. Six stopped at EDA, four at preprocessing, two wrote no artifacts, and one trained a model it did not save. Their reasoning is on disk and much of it is good—the leakage findings in stage 01 are better than what stage 05 acted on—but reasoning that produces no artifact cannot be scored.
