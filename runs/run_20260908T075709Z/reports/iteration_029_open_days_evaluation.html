Primary Test
2021 Holdout — 362 days, 3% closure rate

Primary Test Results: Model vs Corrected Seasonal Naive

The primary test covers January–December 2021 (362 days, 11 zero-sales days, 3% closure). The naive baseline is the seasonal median of the last W same-day-of-week observations before the origin date, where W varies by horizon (W=4 for h≤14, W=26 for h=30/60, W=13 for h=90). The model beats naive on 4 of 6 horizons; h=14 and h=90 remain above the naive floor.

Horizon (h) Val MAE (fold 3) Model MAE (primary) Naive MAE (corrected) Beats Naive? Naive Window (W)
h = 1 29.8 39.3 39.8 ✓ 4 weeks
h = 7 31.6 41.2 41.3 ✓ 4 weeks
h = 14 34.8 44.9 41.8 ✗ 4 weeks
h = 30 36.6 43.0 43.3 ✓ 26 weeks
h = 60 36.2 46.3 49.3 ✓ 26 weeks
h = 90 56.5 64.9 51.8 ✗ 13 weeks

h=14: The 4-week same-DOW window (W=4) is a very strong prior for a 14-day-ahead forecast — it looks back at exactly the same weekday 1, 2, 3, and 4 weeks ago, capturing the recent weekly pattern tightly. The model uses the same anchor via the ratio_correct transform but adds lag-14 and other features that introduce additional estimation error. At 14-day horizons, the seasonal naive's simplicity is a genuine advantage.

h=90: The h=90 model had only 5 scoreable validation rows in fold 3, making hyperparameter selection statistically uncertain. The model uses direct (non-ratio) prediction in absolute units, which is harder to calibrate over a 90-day window where the underlying demand trend has moved. The naive's 13-week lookback window (W=13) effectively averages the same quarter last year — a very solid seasonal reference that the model does not consistently beat.

Stress Test — Distribution Shift
Key Finding

37.6% of Stress Test Days Have Zero Bread Sales — Store Closures, Not Demand Collapse

The stress test (2022–2023) shows dramatically higher raw MAEs than the primary test. The root cause is not degraded model quality: 65 of 173 stress test days (37.6%) have zero bread sales, indicating store closure, compared to just 3% in the primary test and 1–7% during the training years. On these closure days, any demand forecast is meaningless — the store does not operate.

distribution_shift — Monthly sales timeline (left), DOW breakdown including closures (centre), DOW on open days only + closure rate (right). On open days, stress demand is consistent with the training trend.

The bakery shifted to a weekend-dominant operating model in 2022–2023, closing on most weekdays. The closure rate by day-of-week in the stress period: Saturday 0%, Sunday ~11%, Monday–Thursday 38–48%, Friday 65%. This pattern did not exist in the training data (training closure rate: 1–7% across all years). The COVID-19 lockdown (March–June 2020) was explicitly excluded from the training set, which means the model was never exposed to any sustained consecutive-closure sequence. As a result, it cannot reliably predict whether a given day is open or closed — but more importantly, in production the model would not be called for days known to be closed. Open-days-only evaluation is therefore the correct production-aligned metric.

Closure Evolution
Training → Primary → Stress

Closure Rate Jumped from 1–7% During Training to 37–38% in the Stress Period

During the training years (2010–2020), the zero-sales closure rate ranged from 1% to 7% per year — primarily public holidays and occasional closures. The primary test year (2021) had a 3% closure rate, consistent with training. The stress period (2022–2023) represents a fundamental operational shift: the store adopted a weekend-dominant schedule that the model was never exposed to during training.

closure_evolution — Yearly closure rate (left), monthly timeline (centre), DOW comparison across periods (right). The 2022–2023 jump is a regime shift, not gradual drift.

Note: The COVID lockdown year (2020) had a 33% closure rate but was explicitly excluded from model training. The model therefore has no learned representation of high-closure-rate regimes.

Stress Test — Open Days
Correct Production-Aligned Evaluation

Model Beats Corrected Naive on All 6 Horizons When Evaluated on Open Days Only

In production, the forecasting model is only called for days expected to be open. Evaluating on closure days inflates MAE artificially — any non-zero forecast is wrong when the store is closed with zero sales. Filtering to open-days-only (n_open = 30–53 per horizon) gives the correct picture: the model beats the corrected seasonal naive on every horizon.

The largest win is at h=14 (model 48.4 vs naive 87.7 — a 45% improvement). The naive's short lookback window (W=4) for h=14 picks up zeros from nearby closure days in its 4-week median, severely under-estimating demand. The model's ratio transform and longer-horizon features are less contaminated by surrounding closure days.

Diagnostics
Residual Degradation Explained

h=1 and h=7 Stress Open-Day MAEs (93.6, 86.6) Remain High Due to Lag Contamination

Even on open days, the h=1 and h=7 model MAEs are much higher than their primary-test equivalents (39.3 and 41.2 respectively). The mechanism is lag feature contamination: the model's short-horizon lag features (lag_1d, lag_7d) look back at the immediately preceding days. In the stress period, those adjacent days frequently have zero bread sales (closure days). When the model sees a zero in lag_1d or lag_7d, it interprets that as a signal of low near-term demand — but on an open day, the store is operating normally and demand returns to typical levels.

This is the same root cause as the naive's degradation at h=1,7 — the W=4 lookback window for the naive also picks up zeros — but the model's lag features are even more directly affected because they reference single prior days rather than a median over 4 weeks.

Why naive is also bad at h=1,7 on stress open days

The seasonal naive for h=1 uses W=4 (4-week lookback). In the stress period, those 4 same-DOW weeks may include 1–3 zero-sales days, pulling the median down to zero or near-zero. The model and naive share the same contamination mechanism — but the model's margin over naive is preserved because both are impacted roughly equally.

At h=14, the model's primary lag feature looks back 14 days (bread_lag_14d). Fourteen days ago, the closure pattern is less likely to fall exactly on that same weekday — the contamination is diluted over a longer lookback. Additionally, the h=14 model uses a ratio_correct transform anchored to a longer-horizon seasonal median, which is more robust to a single zero entry. This is why h=14's model MAE (48.4) is far below the naive's (87.7): the naive's W=4 window almost certainly includes at least one zero-sales same-DOW day in the stress period, while the model's longer features are more insulated.

Summary
Evaluation Summary

Summary and Known Limitations

Strengths

  • Model beats corrected seasonal naive on 4 of 6 primary horizons (2021, stable conditions).
  • Model beats corrected naive on all 6 horizons on stress open days — the production-aligned metric.
  • Large margin at h=14 on stress open days (model 48.4 vs naive 87.7) — ratio transform and longer lags are robust to closure contamination.
  • h=30 and h=60 perform comparably to primary test on stress open days, showing good long-horizon generalisation.
  • Naive baseline correctly defined with target-DOW (not origin-DOW) — closing a historical bug in iteration 027.

Known Limitations

  • h=14 and h=90 do not beat naive on the primary test. The seasonal naive is a strong baseline at 14-day horizons; h=90 has high estimation variance due to 5-row fold-3 training signal.
  • h=1 and h=7 degrade significantly on stress open days due to lag contamination from surrounding closure days. A two-stage model (closure classifier + quantity forecaster) would address this.
  • Stress test sample is small (n_open = 30–53 per horizon). Margins at h=30 (−0.1) and h=1 (−0.8) are not statistically meaningful at this sample size.
  • Model never saw the weekend-dominant closure regime in training. COVID lockdown was excluded from training, so no consecutive-closure sequences were learned.
  • 2022 data is 35% complete (missing XMLs); the stress evaluation relies primarily on January–February 2023.

Recommended Future Work

A two-stage model — (1) a closure classifier predicting whether each future day is open or closed, followed by (2) the existing demand forecaster conditioned on open days — would fully decouple the closure and demand problems. Additionally, retraining with a recent-closure-rate feature (rolling 30-day closure rate in the lookback window) would allow the model to adapt to the weekend-dominant regime without requiring explicit closure-day labels in the feature set.
